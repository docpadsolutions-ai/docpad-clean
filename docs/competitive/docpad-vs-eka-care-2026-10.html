Competitor analysis · 16 October 2026 · prepared before Phase-2 scoping

We are not losing on features. We are losing on finishing.

Eka Care and DocPad were both mapped screen by screen on 15–16 October. Eka is built so a solo doctor can run a whole OPD day in about eight clicks and the patient leaves with a prescription on WhatsApp. DocPad is built so nothing unsafe or unauditable can ever reach the database — and then takes roughly twenty clicks to get there, and sends the patient a link with the diagnosis missing.

How these positions were decided
  • Finishing the job — Eka's walk-in to finished prescription was executed end to end on a test patient: queue, check-in, vitals, pad, PDF, automatic WhatsApp, receipt, follow-up booking. DocPad's documentation flow is complete, but the patient-facing prescription link returns no diagnosis, no advice and no follow-up date, and appointment reminders depend on a scheduled-job secret the repo's own notes say was never set.
  • Breadth — DocPad has inpatient care (around 35 tables including medication administration and an accreditation checklist), pharmacy with an 821-line formulary and a stock ledger, insurance preauthorisation and claims, and tax-aware billing. None of these were found in the mapped Eka product; its inpatient module was an unlicensed sales demo. Against that, roughly a third of DocPad's 42 mapped routes never resolved, were stubs, or were labelled coming soon.
  • Speed — counted, not judged. See the friction section.
  • Clinical safety — DocPad's architecture is the strongest thing in either product: a database trigger that refuses an unsafe prescription, a cross-reactivity map across drug families, and 26 automated assertions. It is rated only adequate because those checks do not yet fire on data entered through the app — see the gap below. Eka showed a severity label and nothing more on the one interacting pair tested, and no allergy list was found anywhere in the mapped product.
  • Readiness to sell — Eka publishes prices and, per a November 2025 interview with its founder, had roughly 12,000 paying doctors. DocPad has no self-serve signup, no screen anywhere that creates a new hospital, and its national health-ID enrolment still returns a hardcoded placeholder.

The five things worth repeating in a room

What actually stands out

01 — our strongest position, and not yet safe to sell

We built the better safety system. It is not switched on.

No allergy list was found anywhere in the Eka product we mapped. Its medical history is six toggles — diabetes, hypertension, thyroid, alcohol, tobacco, smoking — so it has nothing to check a prescription against. On the one interacting pair tested it showed a severity label and a drug pair: no mechanism, no consequence, no advice, and no prompt to record a reason for overriding. DocPad's answer is far better designed: a database trigger that refuses the prescription outright, management advice on screen, and a cross-reactivity map that knows a penicillin allergy implicates cephalosporins.

But on DocPad today that hard stop almost never fires. The only screen that records an allergy writes to an older free-text field, which the checker treats as an unclassified substance — and an unclassified substance is never allowed to block. The cephalosporin match is advisory and does not block either. Behind it, the interaction content is, by the repository's own September note, about 24 drug pairs, with warfarin plus aspirin returning clean.

So what: still the one claim a competitor cannot answer with a sprint — but do not make it in a sales conversation until the entry form feeds the checker and the content is real. Today we would be claiming a capability our own test fixtures show is dormant.

02 — they win this outright

Eka finishes the visit. DocPad finishes the note.

On Eka, about eight interactions take a patient from the queue to a finished prescription that has already been sent to their WhatsApp, with a receipt raised at check-in and the follow-up booking offered on the same screen. On DocPad the same stretch takes roughly twenty, prescribing happens in a modal rather than on the page, and the patient then receives a link that omits the diagnosis, the advice and the follow-up date — the three things they most need.

So what: a doctor seeing 60 patients a day spends about 1,200 interactions getting through the list, against roughly 500 on Eka. It is the single biggest reason a clinic would choose them over us.

03 — the capability gap that compounds

Their software gets faster the longer you use it. Ours doesn't.

Eka's "My Genie" learns each doctor's own prescribing — dose, frequency, timing, duration, instructions — and replays it as defaults, with per-attribute controls over exactly what is remembered. It had already learned two medicines after a single test prescription. DocPad has most of the same machinery: a medication history table, a favourites star, and a working similarity search over past prescriptions. But the prefill only fires from one tab, and the similarity result is a read-only grey box the doctor cannot apply.

So what: this is the cheapest large win on the board. The parts exist and are not plugged in.

04 — the wedge

Eka's own users are voting for things we already built

Eka runs a public feature-request board where its users vote. Top items when it was read: a QR code on printed prescriptions (101 votes), bulk messaging (65), regional-language follow-up messages (56), an e-signature (53), consent forms (45), inpatient support (37). Two of those six are things DocPad already has — a consent template library with mandatory and optional forms, and a full inpatient module with medication administration and discharge summaries. The other four are gaps we share with them.

So what: a public, vote-counted list of what Eka's own users are asking for and not getting. Two entries are ours to exploit today. The other four say where the whole category is weak, which is also worth knowing.

05 — the thing to not build a plan on

The government incentive story is shrinking, for both of us

Eka sells a dashboard that tracks rupees earned from the national Digital Health Incentive Scheme. The scheme's seventh revision, dated April 2026, narrowed it hard: only identity-verified records count, the share-a-QR route no longer earns anything, a clinic must clear 100 eligible transactions in a month before it is paid anything at all, and a routine prescription now pays the facility ₹5. That document set a window to September 2026, and no extension notice could be found.

So what: do not let incentive income become a pillar of the DocPad business case. At those rates a busy solo clinic clears a few thousand rupees a month, and a clinic under the 100-transaction floor earns nothing at all.

The gaps that matter

The order is the recommendation. The first three are small jobs with outsized effect; the last two are strategic commitments.

The patient never receives the diagnosis

BlocksSmall

DocPad's patient-facing prescription link returns vitals, the chief complaint and the medicines — but not the diagnosis, not the advice, and not the follow-up date. A patient told to come back in three days has no record of it. The WhatsApp message carries a medication block, so the instruction survives only if the doctor happened to type it there.

Who it hurtsEvery patient, and the doctor whose follow-up never arrives
Why smallOne database function decides what the link returns
Also fixThe link is a permanent unguessable address with no expiry

There is no front door

BlocksMedium

No signup. No screen that creates a hospital — a new customer currently needs someone to run SQL. No public booking page, no embeddable widget, no clinic QR poster, no doctor profile. Eka's entire acquisition engine is exactly these surfaces, and they double as the patient's way in. Without them DocPad cannot be sold to a clinic at all, only delivered to one.

One thing to copy carefully, not wholesale

Eka's automatic send is also its worst design decision. Finishing a prescription dispatches it to the patient with no confirmation step and no undo, and bills the clinic for the message. A doctor who finishes the wrong draft has already sent it. Their settings do let a clinic choose which messages go out on which channel, so this may be configurable — but on the account we mapped, finishing sent it. Copy the speed, not the absence of a brake: an explicit send, or a few seconds of undo, is both safer and a fair thing to point at in a sales conversation.

Other friction observed on each side
  • DocPad — the encounter screen renders a second navigation header inside the main sidebar, so there are two competing navigations on the most important screen. The billing module renders dark panels while the rest of the app is light, and on the insurance page the heading is dark text on a dark surface and effectively invisible. Marking a visit complete requires no complaint, no diagnosis and no medication, despite the complaint field showing a required marker. A Save Draft button in the prescription modal has no handler attached.
  • Eka — the queue row packs about ten actions into a single line, and at a normal laptop size several screens already clip. Support chat bubbles and product coach-marks repeatedly covered primary controls during mapping. Coded diagnoses are off by default, so most of its data is uncoded unless a doctor finds the setting. Its own "assessment summary" showed nothing after a completed questionnaire.
  • Both — desktop-first. Eka already clips on several screens at 1440×727, and the mapping recommends treating 1280px and up as the design target for any rebuild. DocPad's encounter screen has exactly one layout switch and no tablet work, with a dosage popover fixed at 420px that is tight on a ten-inch tablet held upright.

The business factor that outweighs any feature

On those November 2025 figures Eka had converted under a fifth of its doctors to a paid plan, and earned roughly ₹25–29 crore a year against losses, having raised nothing new since 2022. That reads as a company under pressure to monetise rather than one with room to out-build a competitor. It makes the market attackable — but it also means price alone will not win it, because a government-backed clinic product at ₹499 a month already sits below everyone. The way in is being visibly better at something a doctor feels in the first week.

Why the two products differ

Two different bets about what a clinician wants

Neither company has said this. These are the bets each product's behaviour implies, and almost every difference above follows from them. Neither is wrong.

Eka believes

The doctor is the customer, the prescription is the product, and whoever holds the national record holds the market.

So the pad is fast, personal and forgiving. The identity wizard opens before manual entry, which is what you would build if the record were the asset. Breadth ships across five separate web properties with three different visual languages, as if being present everywhere beat being seamless. And the safety features inform rather than interrupt — which is the shape you get when the person you answer to is the one being interrupted.

DocPad believes

The institution is the customer, the database is the product, and anything that can be bypassed will be.

So safety rules live in the database where no screen can route around them, the audit log cannot be edited even by the system, and tenant isolation is proven by automated tests. Breadth means one application covering outpatients, inpatients, pharmacy, billing and insurance together. The cost of that bet is visible in every click count on this page: the product was designed to be correct before it was designed to be quick.

The useful thing is that these bets are not actually in conflict. A hard stop that takes one keystroke to satisfy is still a hard stop. Nothing about enforcing safety at the database requires twenty clicks — that is a separate, fixable choice about the interface on top of it.

Honest ending

What would change the call

Whether our own safety checks actually fire

This is the first thing to settle, because the verdict leans on it. Two questions for whoever owns the clinical code: does an allergy entered through the encounter screen today reach the hard stop, and how many drug pairs are really in the live interaction table? The repository's own September note says about 24, and that warfarin with aspirin comes back clean. Until both are answered, the safety advantage is architectural rather than clinical.

If Eka ships an allergy list

The clinical-safety advantage narrows sharply, because their drug knowledge base is visibly broader than ours — branded Indian products mapped to generics and manufacturers — even though their interaction content was never sized. Watch their public feature board; it would appear there first.

If the live instance is healthier than it looked

The five high-severity defects and the stuck screens were observed on 15 October on the Vercel instance, while the source read is from 19 September. If the defects are environment problems rather than product problems, the readiness position improves and nothing else on this page changes.

If the incentive scheme is renewed generously

Then interoperability moves up both tracks rather than waiting for a deal. One official notice settles it.

Questions only your team can answer

Who is the first clinic-tier customer, and do they have inpatient beds? Is anyone paying for DocPad today, and what did they buy? And the one that decides Track 1 entirely: is there appetite to support self-serve customers, who arrive without an implementation and expect the software to explain itself?

Eka's source code, its inpatient module (not licensed on the account), its ambient scribe (a separate application), and its administration and analytics properties beyond their structure. On DocPad, no write actions were performed, so prescribing, billing and registration were documented from the code rather than executed. Drug-interaction content lives only in the live database and could not be counted.
